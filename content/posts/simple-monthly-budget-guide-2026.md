A useful budget is a plan for the money you actually have—not a set of percentages you have to follow perfectly. Start with your take-home income, list the expenses you can verify, and make a plan that reflects your current needs and priorities.

## 1. Start with take-home income

Write down the income you expect to receive during the month after taxes and payroll deductions. If your income changes from week to week, use a cautious estimate based on recent months rather than assuming every month will be your best one.

Keep occasional or uncertain income separate from money you rely on for essential bills. You can decide how to use it once it arrives.

## 2. List the expenses you can confirm

Review recent bank or card statements, bills, and receipts. Group your spending into a few categories that are easy for you to understand:

- **Regular essentials:** housing, utilities, insurance, transportation, and minimum debt payments.
- **Flexible spending:** groceries, household items, fuel, and personal spending.
- **Goals and irregular costs:** savings, extra debt payments, annual fees, repairs, and other costs that do not happen every month.

Include less frequent expenses by estimating their monthly share. For example, if an annual bill is $600, setting aside $50 per month can help you plan for it.

## 3. Choose priorities that fit your situation

After listing income and expenses, decide what needs attention first. That might mean covering essential bills, building a small emergency cushion, paying down debt, or saving for a known upcoming cost.

The 50/30/20 rule—often described as needs, wants, and savings or debt goals—can be a starting point for some households. It is not a requirement or a measure of success. Local costs, income, health needs, family responsibilities, and debt can make a different balance more realistic.

## 4. Give each dollar a job

Subtract planned spending from take-home income. If the plan is short, look for a specific adjustment you can manage, such as changing a flexible category or reviewing a recurring charge. Avoid cutting an essential expense or taking on risky debt just to make a spreadsheet balance.

If money remains, choose where you want it to go before the month starts. You could direct it toward a savings goal, an irregular bill, or additional debt payments, depending on your priorities.

## 5. Check in once a week

Spend a few minutes comparing your plan with what has actually happened. Note upcoming bills and update estimates when you learn what something really costs. A budget is a working plan; changing it when circumstances change is part of using it.

If you share finances with someone else, agree on the categories and goals together. Choose a method you will both maintain, whether that is a notes app, a spreadsheet, or a budgeting tool.

## Quick monthly checklist

1. Estimate take-home income conservatively.
2. Include regular bills, flexible costs, and irregular expenses.
3. Choose a few priorities and check actual spending during the month.
